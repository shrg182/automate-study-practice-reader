The Russia–Ukraine War: Developments to Watch

Three Levels of Conflict

By late August 2026, the Russia–Ukraine war was increasingly being fought on three levels at once: the ground campaign in Donetsk, large drone and missile attacks on cities and logistics, and long-range strikes against economic infrastructure.

1. The Eastern Front

Russia’s principal ground objective remained the Ukrainian-held part of Donetsk Oblast, particularly the fortified urban chain around Kostiantynivka, Druzhkivka, Kramatorsk, and Sloviansk. Russian forces were reported to be infiltrating parts of Kostiantynivka in small infantry groups, while Ukrainian forces continued holding other sectors.

Infiltration is not the same as control. Even the capture of one city would not automatically collapse the wider defensive belt. The important question is whether local pressure can become an operational breakthrough toward the larger Kramatorsk–Sloviansk area.

2. A More Intense Air Campaign

Russia sharply increased the duration and scale of drone and missile attacks in the final days of August. The attacks targeted ports, industrial sites, energy facilities, warehouses, and transport infrastructure, placing additional pressure on Ukraine’s limited air defenses.

On August 29, Ukrainian authorities said a Russian drone had struck an ammunition warehouse in Myla, west of Kyiv. The strike caused secondary explosions and fires that killed at least 37 people and damaged nearby homes and a care facility. President Volodymyr Zelenskyy also called for an investigation into the storage of explosives near residential buildings. The disaster therefore involved both the initial attack and the consequences of unsafe ammunition storage.

3. Pressure on Ukraine’s Logistics

Recent Russian attacks increasingly focused on the systems that allow ordinary economic life to continue: ports, railways, postal terminals, warehouses, retail distribution centers, and energy facilities.

Ukraine’s agriculture minister said that attacks had destroyed about 90 percent of the food-logistics capacity operated by major retail chains. This did not mean that 90 percent of Ukraine’s food supply had disappeared. It referred to a particular part of the modern distribution network. The distinction is important because dramatic percentages can mislead when their scope is omitted.

This pattern can be understood as an economic attrition strategy: damaging distribution and infrastructure in order to raise costs, exhaust defenses, and make normal life harder to sustain.

4. Ukrainian Strikes Inside Russia

Ukraine was also expanding long-range drone attacks against Russian territory. Oil refineries and fuel infrastructure were especially important targets because damage to them could create domestic shortages and reduce petroleum revenue.

Refinery damage → lower fuel output → pressure on domestic supply and exports → higher economic costs.

The effectiveness and durability of this campaign remained uncertain, but its strategic logic was clear. Relatively inexpensive drones could force the use of costly air-defense missiles or temporarily interrupt facilities worth billions of dollars.

5. The Changing Technology of War

The conflict continued moving beyond the familiar combination of trenches, artillery, and armored vehicles. Cheap short-range drones, long-range unmanned aircraft, missiles, electronic warfare, satellite intelligence, and air-defense systems were becoming central to operations.

Ukrainian officials discussed a major increase in long-range drone production and use. Such targets should be treated as ambitions, not as proof of sustainable capacity. Production rates, access to components, operator training, and the opponent’s countermeasures all affect what can actually be achieved.

6. Foreign Military Support

Ukraine continued to depend heavily on foreign assistance, particularly for air-defense ammunition. Reports also described British approval for sharing classified technical information connected with possible assembly of Storm Shadow cruise missiles in Ukraine.

Local assembly could reduce dependence on irregular foreign deliveries, but production would still require specialized components, financing, security, and time. Russia, meanwhile, continued warning that deeper Western involvement could lead to retaliation. Such warnings form part of the conflict’s broader effort at deterrence.

7. Diplomacy Without a Settlement

Ukraine’s presidential chief of staff, Kyrylo Budanov, said Ukrainian and Russian teams might hold talks in September with American representatives participating. CIA Director John Ratcliffe also visited Moscow and met Russian intelligence officials, although the Kremlin said he did not meet President Vladimir Putin.

These contacts did not remove the main obstacle. Russia continued demanding Ukrainian withdrawal from territory in the Donbas that it had not captured, while Ukraine rejected voluntarily surrendering land it still controlled. Technical talks can reopen communication without producing an immediate political settlement.

9. An Attritional War

The war did not appear close to a conventional military conclusion. Russia held the initiative in parts of Donetsk but had not achieved the rapid breakthrough needed to dismantle Ukraine’s larger defensive system. Ukraine, meanwhile, demonstrated an ability to impose costs far behind the front.

Both countries were therefore trying to move the war beyond the immediate battlefield. Russia targeted Ukrainian logistics, food distribution, electricity, ports, and industry. Ukraine targeted Russian refineries, fuel distribution, logistics, and military production.

10. Questions to Watch

  1. Can Russia turn pressure around Kostiantynivka into a wider breakthrough?
  2. Can Ukrainian refinery attacks cause sustained fuel disruption or materially reduce Russian revenue?
  3. Will anticipated negotiations become substantive political talks, or remain technical discussions while both sides prepare for winter?
