The Week in 90 Words President Donald Trump attended a NATO summit in Ankara. NATO members discussed higher defense spending and more military production. In the United States, a housing bill became law even though Trump did not sign it. He also changed trade rules for imported aircraft and parts. The Justice Department sent subpoenas to New York Times reporters after a story about Air Force One security. Trump also threatened a major military response if Iran assassinated him. Together, these events show Trump's use of pressure in foreign policy, trade, Congress, and public communication. Main Ideas • NATO is moving from promises about spending toward production and delivery. • A president can let a bill become law without signing it if the constitutional conditions are met. • Trade actions can help some industries but raise costs for others. • Subpoenas to journalists create questions about security, sources, and press freedom. • Strong threats may discourage an enemy, but they can also increase tension.

Trump Recent News | English Study Edition | July 12, 2026 Page 2 1. Five News Stories in Plain English NATO summit in Ankara NATO leaders met on July 7 and 8. They again promised to defend one another under Article 5. NATO said European allies and Canada were taking more responsibility for defense. The White House highlighted new projects involving missiles, aircraft, and production in Europe. The important question is whether governments will turn spending promises into equipment that can be delivered on time. Key distinction: A summit announcement is a beginning, not a finished result. Housing law without a signature Trump chose not to sign a bipartisan housing bill, but he also did not veto it. The measure therefore became law. Trump said he refused to sign because the Senate had not passed the voter-identification legislation he wanted. The event connected two different issues—housing and election rules—and showed tension between the White House and Congress. Key distinction: The law took effect; the political disagreement continued. Trade action on aircraft Trump adjusted the treatment of imported commercial aircraft, engines, and parts. The administration said the action would protect national security and strengthen American aerospace production. However, aircraft supply chains cross many countries. The policy could support domestic companies, but it could also affect costs, repairs, and delivery times. Key distinction: The details, exemptions, and reactions will determine the effect. Journalists received subpoenas The Justice Department subpoenaed New York Times journalists after they reported security concerns involving the new Air Force One. A subpoena is a legal order to provide testimony or evidence. News organizations worry that forcing reporters to identify sources can discourage important reporting. The government's complete legal argument was not public when this report was prepared. Key distinction: A subpoena is not proof that a reporter committed a crime. A threat involving Iran Trump said that orders existed for a very large U.S. attack if Iran assassinated him. The statement was meant as deterrence: a warning designed to stop an attack. If a president died, however, the next president would become commander in chief and make military decisions. Strong public threats can deter an enemy, but they can also create misunderstanding or escalation. Key distinction: This was a warning, not a report that an attack had happened.
